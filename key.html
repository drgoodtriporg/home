Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy

Study summary of BrainFutures’ August 2022 elements report. Informational only — not medical advice. Read the report (PDF).

Screening and assessment

  • Does this body, history, and drug list fit psychedelic-assisted therapy?
  • A finding can rule out one compound and leave another open.

Medical and symptom review

  • Physiological and psychological screen for conditions that could complicate treatment.
  • CAPS-5 (PTSD) and MADRS (depression): baseline, then repeat after treatment.
  • Cardiac conditions are major physical contraindications.
  • Check other drugs for interactions.
  • Many therapists require antidepressants stopped six to eight weeks before dosing.

Trauma

  • Screening includes a trauma assessment: what may surface in preparation and in the session.
  • Psychedelics often bring traumatic material forward. Trauma-informed skill is part of screening.

Exclusions

  • Common research exclusions: psychosis, and a family history of psychosis.
  • Schizophrenia and bipolar disorder are compound-dependent.
  • Heart-related cautions also differ by compound. Snapshot on Safety.

Preparation

  • Trust, education, informed consent, safety measures.
  • Research trials: about three 90-minute sessions.
  • Complex history may need more. Phelps: some therapists insist on four to ten hours in very difficult cases before considering the medicine.

Therapeutic alliance

  • Trust lets the patient surrender. Without it, trying to control the session can become overwhelming.
  • Rapport below a threshold: consider delaying the medication session (Carhart-Harris).
  • Consciously “choose to trust.” Speak up about any rupture, however small (Brian Richards).

Inner healing and surrender

  • Inner healing intelligence (Mithoefer): “a person’s innate capacity to heal the wounds of trauma.”
  • Therapists extend it beyond trauma, including to depression. The patient finds what helps.
  • Mantras: Bill Richards’ “Trust, let go, and be open”; Ann Shulgin’s “Enter into it and look out through its eyes.”
  • Also taught: intention and breath.
  • Meet shame, guilt, fear, anxiety, or grief directly.
  • Field phrases: “the only way out is through”; “trust yourself and trust the medicine.”
  • Reminder in the moment: a medicine was taken, and this will pass.

What the session can be like

  • Range cannot be predicted. Getty’s three kinds, often mixed:
    • Deep emotional — can be hard.
    • Clearer, insightful.
    • Mystical or transpersonal.
  • Intense emotion is possible. Physical risk in a supervised setting is described as low.
  • Not a miracle cure. Set that limit here.
  • Sense of self, and close relationships, may shift.

Trauma history in preparation

  • MAPS MDMA manual (Mithoefer, 2017): do not ask for a detailed trauma account.
  • If the patient offers it, listen.
  • Ask what would make the experience safe, apart from the trauma.
  • Screening still assesses trauma. This restraint is for preparation.

Consent, touch, and the days before

  • Informed consent is ongoing. The signed form is not the end.
  • Withdrawable at any time, no reason required (Penn).
  • Research consent (Yale manual, cited): attend prep and integration; diet and drug limits; stay in the room; report suicidal thoughts or imminent self-harm.
  • Agree touch beforehand. Example: hand, or hand on shoulder; a hug may be declined. Therapist also names touch they will not offer.
  • Touch consent can be revoked in the session. No touch agreed: sitting nearby can still support.
  • Some clinicians state the therapy is never sexual.
  • Light low-fat breakfast, or fast (possible nausea). Comfortable clothes in layers.
  • No driving. Support person for pickup and aftercare.
  • Days before and just after: lower stress; no alcohol or other drugs; review medication changes.
  • Seeing the treatment room ahead of time can help.

Set and setting

  • Set: mindset and expectations brought to the session.
  • Setting: physical and social environment.
  • Both run from screening onward and shape the medication session.

Set

  • Expectations, motivations, intentions, mood.
  • Also the day’s stress and physical condition.
  • Aim: calm and reflective.
  • Acute distress: consider postponing. Agitation or ill health is thought to make a hard experience more likely.

Setting

  • Welcoming interpersonal atmosphere.
  • Often living-room-like: couches, soft light, objects that put the person at ease.
  • Familiar, and physically and psychologically safe.
  • Personal items: photograph, blanket, stuffed animal.
  • Eyeshades and headphones. Music supports inward focus, emotional release, imagery, and reassurance.
  • Music-evoked emotion is described as significantly enhanced under psychedelics. Experts interviewed treat music as vital in treatment settings.
  • Secure room, few interruptions, emergency care within reach.

Medication session

  • Supervised. Often six to eight hours, until effects wear off.
  • Therapist role: support and safety, including effects that need medical care.
  • Stance: highly nondirective.

Surrender in the session

  • “Accept whatever comes up in the moment,” as if it is what is supposed to happen (Cosimano).
  • Stay with shame, guilt, fear, anxiety, or grief.
  • “The only way out is through.”
  • The useful answer is the one the patient finds.

Nondirective support

  • Midwifery (Bill Richards): step in to reassure; otherwise “turn off the rational mind.”
  • Do not label or pathologize the experience as it happens.
  • Largely silent unless the patient engages, plus check-ins and medical monitoring.
  • When ordinary consciousness returns: more dialogue, and often a journal of salient moments for integration.

When the session is hard

  • Grounding presence, or touch already agreed, for anxiety or panic.
  • Blood pressure and heart rate on a schedule.
  • Research settings may also include a physician on site, or emergency care and rescue medication; after-hours therapist contact; control of who enters and when the patient may leave.

Meaning

  • High trial doses can bring ego dissolution.
  • Experiences are often later ranked among the most meaningful of a life.
  • A mystical or spiritual experience is welcomed as one factor that may support healing.

Integration

  • Move session insights into daily life.
  • Research protocols: three or more sessions, about four to seven hours total.
  • Spread over a few weeks. Often starts the morning after dosing.
  • Outside a short trial, can continue as long as it helps.

In the sessions

  • Dosing is generally nondirective. Integration may be more directive.
  • Named examples: Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), Internal Family Systems (IFS).
  • Some protocols stay patient-led. COMPASS: “method of inquiry.”
  • Use the journal and recall.
  • Keep the treated condition central — depression, PTSD, or whatever brought the person — along with the session content (Walker).

After dosing

  • On-call therapist.
  • Overwhelm, anxiety, or depression in the following days is not uncommon, and often eases with support.
  • Symptoms can also worsen more seriously, including when traumatic memories return, and may need intensive outpatient care.
  • Ontological shock: a sudden challenge to what is real, and to who one is.
  • Can follow ego dissolution, a mystical experience, or a vision of someone who has died.
  • Relationships may change. The old “script” may no longer fit.
  • Hold major life decisions for the weeks right after.
  • Repeat the screening scales to track the condition.

Safety and ethical practice

  • Foundational, and present in every phase.
  • Covers psychological risk, physiological risk, and therapist misconduct.
  • Overview, not a practice manual. Appendix I below is not exhaustive.

Psychological

  • Common acute risks: anxiety; psychotic thinking (confusion, thought disorder, paranoia). Usually resolve within a few hours.
  • Watch suicidality, especially in early integration.
  • Whether this treatment raises or lowers suicide risk is not settled. Many candidates are already at elevated risk from the condition being treated.
  • Patients need a way to reach help, including emergency care if self-harm is imminent.
  • HPPD, among users: brief flashbacks (Type 1) about 1 in 20; distressing visual disturbances over months to years (Type 2) about 1 in 50,000.
  • HPPD risk is believed much lower in clinical settings. Mechanism is not well understood.

Spiritual emergency

  • Some complex responses persist and can present as psychosis, mania, or suicidality.
  • Cardenas: specialized support in a psychedelic safety net, “in line with Stanislav Grof’s idea of a spiritual emergency.”
  • Can look just like psychiatric illness.
  • Separate from medical complications, which need medical attention.
  • For this presentation, a psychiatric hospital can sometimes do more harm.
  • His estimate: up to one person in 1,000 or 2,000 cases.
  • Weigh the risk of triggering or worsening psychosis in informed consent.
  • No conclusive evidence linking psychedelics to persisting psychosis. Studies still exclude psychosis and a family history of psychosis.

Physiological

  • For healthy patients under supervision, physical harm from most psychedelics is described as low next to many common substances, such as alcohol.
  • Serious or lasting physical harm appears uncommon.
  • Medical clearance first, including undiagnosed heart conditions.
  • In session: blood pressure and heart rate may rise. Headache, nausea, and fatigue are common and usually pass.
  • Which risks matter depends on the compound.

Conduct

  • Dosing is a highly vulnerable state: harder to assert a boundary, resist, or leave.
  • Named risks: sexual misconduct, financial exploitation.
  • Many research trials use two therapists at the medication session, partly against abuse.
  • A second therapist can help against sexual violation, and has still not been enough in every case (Beachy).
  • Audio or video, sometimes with live supervision, is discussed as a further check.
  • Tradeoff: two therapists raise cost and can narrow access. The report asks whether two are needed in every case.
  • Also: the profession’s code of ethics, and therapists in community rather than isolated.

Compound snapshot

  • Appendix I, August 2022. A sketch of differences, not a clearance list.
  • As of this report: esketamine already FDA-approved for major depression, including suicidal ideation with an MDD diagnosis. MDMA and psilocybin still in trials.

Ketamine

  • Contraindicated in schizophrenia; may worsen it.
  • For ketamine, heart failure and hypertension are the primary physical contraindications, especially if a blood-pressure rise would be dangerous.
  • Can raise blood pressure and pulse.
  • Most common neurological effects named: headache, dizziness.

MDMA

  • Can raise heart rate and blood pressure.
  • Phase 2 trials excluded cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease.
  • May worsen bipolar disorder and schizophrenia.
  • Serotonin syndrome risk with MAOIs.
  • Without integration and support, the Mithoefers caution that suicide could be a risk.

Psilocybin

  • Lower toxicity, as described in the report.
  • Usual acute reactions: early nausea and anxiety, tending to ease as effects fade.
  • Heart rate and blood pressure can rise.
  • Psychotic-spectrum illness, including family history, excluded; may aggravate psychosis.
  • Bipolar depression: risk not well understood. Use caution.
