Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy

A study summary of BrainFutures’ August 2022 report on the elements of psychedelic-assisted therapy. Informational only — not medical advice. Read the report (PDF).

Screening and assessment

Screening asks whether psychedelic-assisted therapy fits a person’s body, psychological history, and other drugs. In research trials that screen is strict. A history or a medicine can rule out one compound and still leave another open.

Medical and symptom review

  • The workup is both physiological and psychological, looking for conditions that could complicate treatment.
  • Validated scales such as CAPS-5 for PTSD and MADRS for depression set a baseline. They are repeated after treatment to track symptoms.
  • Cardiac conditions are major physical contraindications and are part of medical screening.
  • Other drugs are reviewed for interactions. Many therapists require patients to be off antidepressants for six to eight weeks before the medication session.

Trauma

Screening does include a trauma assessment. It tells the therapist what may surface later, in preparation and in the medication session. Psychedelics often bring traumatic material forward, so the report treats trauma-informed skill as part of competent screening.

Exclusions

Psychosis, and a family history of psychosis, are common research exclusions, used as a precaution. Schizophrenia and bipolar disorder are compound-dependent: ketamine, MDMA, and psilocybin are not screened the same way. Heart-related cautions differ by compound as well. The Safety tab has the report’s short Appendix I snapshot.

Preparation

Preparation builds trust, teaches what may happen, takes informed consent, and sets safety measures. Research trials typically use about three 90-minute sessions. A complex history may call for more time. Janis Phelps describes therapists who insist on four to ten hours with someone in very difficult straits before they will consider the medicine.

Therapeutic alliance

  • Trust is what lets a patient surrender to the experience. Without it, trying to control the session can make the experience overwhelming.
  • Robin Carhart-Harris, cited in the report, says rapport below a certain threshold is a reason to consider delaying the medication session. A 2022 study including him found that therapeutic alliance ahead of a second psilocybin session had a direct impact on final depression scores.
  • Brian Richards describes trust as a choice the patient can make: consciously “choose to trust,” and speak up about any rupture, however small.

Inner healing and surrender

Michael Mithoefer’s phrase for this stance is inner healing intelligence: “a person’s innate capacity to heal the wounds of trauma.” Therapists have carried the same idea to other diagnoses, including depression. The stance is that the patient finds what helps.

Mantras often used to support that surrender include Bill Richards’ “Trust, let go, and be open” and Ann Shulgin’s “Enter into it and look out through its eyes.” Intention and breath are taught the same way, as ways to stay with the moment.

Patients are encouraged to meet shame, guilt, fear, anxiety, or grief directly. Phrases in the field include “the only way out is through” and “trust yourself and trust the medicine,” aimed at facing the experience rather than intellectualizing it or avoiding it. A therapist may also remind them that they have taken a medicine and that the moment will pass.

What the session can be like

Preparation includes education on how widely sessions differ. Adele Getty’s sketch, quoted in the report, is three broad kinds: a deep emotional experience that can be hard; a clearer, more insightful one; and a mystical or transpersonal state. A person may touch all three, and which mix will appear cannot be predicted. Intense emotion is possible, and the physical risk in a supervised setting is described as low.

Psychedelic-assisted therapy is not a miracle cure. Expectations need that limit. Profound shifts in the sense of self are possible, and close relationships may change with them.

Trauma history in preparation

The MAPS MDMA manual, as BrainFutures cites it (Mithoefer, 2017), tells therapists to refrain from asking patients to describe traumatic history in detail. If the patient offers that account, the therapist should be ready to listen. Asking “what would make this a safe experience,” apart from the trauma itself, is part of the same preparation. This restraint belongs to preparation. It does not replace the trauma assessment done at screening.

Consent, touch, and the days before

  • Informed consent is an ongoing conversation. Signing the form is not the end of it. Andrew Penn, cited in the report, stresses that consent can be withdrawn at any time, with no reason required.
  • Research consent, as in the Yale psilocybin manual the report cites, also covers attending preparation and integration, diet and drug limits, staying in the treatment room, and telling therapists about suicidal thoughts or imminent self-harm.
  • Supportive touch is agreed beforehand. A hand, or a hand on the shoulder, may be welcome, and a hug may not. Therapists also name touch they will not offer. Permission can be revoked during the session. If touch is not agreed, sitting nearby is another way to offer support. Some clinicians say outright that the therapy is never sexual.
  • Eat a light, low-fat breakfast, or fast, because of possible nausea. Wear comfortable clothes in layers.
  • Patients are not permitted to drive themselves. A support person provides the ride home and aftercare.
  • In the days before, and just after, reduce stress and refrain from alcohol and other drugs. Review any change in regular medications. Seeing the treatment room ahead of time can help.

Set and setting

Set is the mindset a person brings. Setting is the physical and social environment. The report treats both as active from screening onward, and as strong shapers of the medication session. Michael Pollan’s short form, quoted there: set is “the mind-set or expectation one brings to the experience,” and setting is “the environment in which it takes place.”

Set

  • Set includes expectations, motivations, intentions, and mood, plus whatever stress or health the person walks in with.
  • The aim on the day is a calm, reflective state. If someone arrives acutely distressed, postponing can be appropriate. Agitation or ill health is thought to make a difficult experience more likely.

Setting

  • The social room should feel welcoming. Johnson and colleagues at Johns Hopkins, cited in the report, say the interpersonal atmosphere is hard to overstate.
  • Research rooms are often living-room-like: comfortable couches, soft lighting, and objects that help a person feel at ease. A familiar place that feels physically and psychologically safe is the goal.
  • Patients often bring something of their own — a photograph, a blanket, a stuffed animal — to personalize the room.
  • Eyeshades and headphones with carefully chosen music support an inward focus. Music is meant to help emotional release, mental imagery, and reassurance. Mendel Kaelen, cited in the report, describes music-evoked emotion as significantly enhanced under psychedelics, and several experts BrainFutures interviewed treat music as vital in treatment settings.
  • The location should be secure, interruptions limited, and emergency care quick to reach.

Medication session

The medicine is given in a supervised appointment that often lasts six to eight hours, until the drug effects have worn off. The therapist’s job is support and safety while the experience unfolds, including watching for physical or mental effects that need medical care. Most experts cited in the report call for a highly nondirective stance.

Surrender in the session

Mary Cosimano, at Johns Hopkins, instructs patients to “accept whatever comes up in the moment,” as though what is happening is exactly what is supposed to happen. Shame, guilt, fear, anxiety, or grief are met by staying with the feeling. That is the same teaching as preparation: “the only way out is through,” with the inner-healer belief that the useful answer is the one the patient finds.

Nondirective support

  • Bill Richards’ picture is midwifery. Step in to reassure, and otherwise “turn off the rational mind”: meet the person where they are, without labeling or pathologizing the experience as it happens.
  • The therapist is largely silent unless the patient engages, aside from periodic check-ins and medical monitoring.
  • Once ordinary waking consciousness returns, more dialogue and reflection can begin. Many practitioners then ask the patient to journal, recalling what they can and marking what mattered, for use in integration.

When the session is hard

  • A grounding presence, or touch that was already agreed, can steady anxiety or panic.
  • Blood pressure and heart rate are checked on a schedule. Research settings may also have a physician on site or emergency care and rescue medication within reach, after-hours contact for the therapists, and control over who enters the room and when a patient may leave.

Meaning

High doses used in trials can bring ego dissolution and experiences people later rank among the most meaningful of their lives. The report welcomes a mystical or spiritual experience as one factor that may support healing.

Integration

Integration is how insights enter daily life. Research protocols typically use three or more psychotherapy sessions, about four to seven hours in all, spread over a few weeks and often starting the morning after dosing. Outside a short trial, integration can continue for as long as it helps.

What the sessions are for

  • During the medication session the stance is generally nondirective. In integration the therapist may be more directive. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) and Internal Family Systems (IFS) are examples the report names. Some programs do the opposite and let the patient lead. COMPASS Pathways’ “method of inquiry” is one of those.
  • Journal notes and recall carry the salient parts of the experience into this work.
  • Barry Walker, cited in the report, wants the diagnosed condition — depression, PTSD, or whatever brought the person — to stay a central topic, along with the content of the session.
  • Patients should be able to reach an on-call therapist. In the days after dosing, feeling overwhelmed, anxious, or depressed is not uncommon, and it often eases when integration support is in place. Symptoms can also worsen in a more serious way, including when traumatic memories return, and sometimes need intensive outpatient care.

After the ground shifts

  • Ontological shock is a sudden challenge to what a person has believed is real, and to who they are. Ego dissolution, a mystical experience, or a vision of someone who has died can bring it on. Relationships at home, at work, and in community can change because the person is no longer on the old “script.”
  • Patients are often encouraged to hold off on major life decisions in the weeks immediately after the session, while a new equilibrium forms.
  • The same symptom scales used at screening can be repeated here, to see whether the condition has moved.

Safety and ethical practice

The report calls safety foundational, and present in every phase. The risks it highlights are psychological, physiological, and harm from therapist misconduct. What follows is that overview, not a practice manual. Appendix I, which the snapshot below condenses, is explicitly not exhaustive.

Psychological

  • The most common psychological risks named are acute anxiety and psychotic thinking, such as confusion, thought disorder, and paranoia. These usually resolve within a few hours of dosing.
  • Suicidality needs watching, particularly in early integration. Whether suicide is increased or reduced by this treatment is not settled, and many people who might seek it are already at elevated risk because of the conditions being treated. Patients need to know how to reach help, including emergency care if self-harm is imminent.
  • Hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD) is a longer-term effect the report flags. Among users, brief flashbacks (Type 1) are estimated at one in 20, and visual disturbances over months to years that cause distress (Type 2) at about one in 50,000. The risk is believed to be much lower in clinical settings. The mechanism is not well understood.

Complex responses after a session can continue and present as psychosis, mania, or suicidality. Alexander Cardenas, MD, quoted in the report, says these need specialized support inside a psychedelic safety net, “in line with Stanislav Grof’s idea of a spiritual emergency” — a presentation that can look just like psychiatric illness. He separates them from medical complications, which need medical attention, and says that keeping this group in a psychiatric hospital can sometimes do more harm. He puts the scale at up to one person in 1,000 or 2,000 cases. The report also tells therapists to weigh, in informed consent, the risk of triggering or worsening a psychotic disorder. Its key takeaway on persisting psychosis is that there is no conclusive evidence of an association with psychedelics, and modern studies have still excluded psychosis and a family history of psychosis as a precaution.

Physiological

For physically healthy patients under supervision, the report describes the risk of physical harm from most psychedelics as low compared with many commonly used substances, such as alcohol. Serious injury or lasting physical harm appears uncommon. Medical clearance still comes first, to catch undiagnosed heart conditions. During the session, blood pressure and heart rate may rise. Headache, nausea, and fatigue are among the common effects and usually pass. Which risks matter depends on the compound.

Conduct

The dosing state is highly vulnerable. A patient may be less able to assert a boundary, resist, or leave. Sexual misconduct and financial exploitation are named risks in the field, and high-profile cases of sexual abuse are part of why the report dwells on prevention.

In many recent research trials, two therapists are present for the medication session, partly to protect patients from abuse. Jamie Beachy, cited in the report, says a second therapist can help protect against sexual violation, and that two have still not been enough in every case. Audio or video recording, sometimes with a supervisor watching live, is discussed as a further check on misconduct. The report also names the tradeoff: two therapists raise the cost and can narrow access, and it asks whether two are necessary for every patient and every circumstance. Alongside staffing, it points to each profession’s code of ethics, and to therapists who stay in community with one another rather than practicing in isolation.

Compound snapshot

From Appendix I of the August 2022 report. As of that writing, esketamine was already FDA-approved for major depression, including suicidal ideation when an MDD diagnosis is present. MDMA and psilocybin were still in trials. This is a sketch of differences, not a clearance list.

Ketamine

Contraindicated in schizophrenia, which it may worsen. For ketamine, heart failure and hypertension are the primary physical contraindications, especially where a rise in blood pressure could be dangerous. Blood pressure and pulse can increase. Headache and dizziness are the most common neurological effects named.

MDMA

Can raise heart rate and blood pressure. Phase 2 trials excluded cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease. It may worsen bipolar disorder and schizophrenia. With MAOIs it carries a risk of serotonin syndrome. Michael and Annie Mithoefer caution that without integration and support, suicide could be a risk.

Psilocybin

Described as lower toxicity. Nausea and anxiety early in the session are the usual acute reactions and tend to ease as the effects fade. Heart rate and blood pressure can rise. Psychotic-spectrum illness, including a family history, has been excluded because symptoms may be aggravated. Risk in bipolar depression is not well understood; the report says prescribers should use caution.
